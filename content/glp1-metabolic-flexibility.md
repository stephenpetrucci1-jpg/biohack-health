<div>
<p>There's no serious debate about whether GLP-1 agonists produce weight loss. The trial data is unambiguous and the effect sizes are among the largest in modern pharmacology.</p>

<p>The harder question is narrower and rarely asked cleanly: do these drugs improve underlying metabolic flexibility, or do they reduce the load on a system that remains as inflexible as it was? That distinction predicts what happens when the drug stops, which turns out to be the thing most people actually want to know.</p>

<h2>What the drugs do</h2>

<p>GLP-1 is an endogenous incretin released from intestinal L-cells in response to nutrients. It has four main actions: enhancing glucose-dependent insulin secretion, suppressing glucagon, slowing gastric emptying, and acting centrally to reduce appetite.</p>

<p>Pharmaceutical agonists are engineered for dramatically extended half-life — minutes for native GLP-1, days for semaglutide and tirzepatide.</p>

<h2>What's settled</h2>

<p><strong>Weight loss.</strong> Semaglutide 2.4 mg produced approximately 15% mean loss over 68 weeks in STEP-1. Tirzepatide 15 mg produced approximately 21% over 72 weeks in SURMOUNT-1. Retatrutide, still investigational, produced 28.3% at 80 weeks in TRIUMPH-1 — covered in detail in our <a href="https://biohackhealth.uk/blog/retatrutide-after-phase-3">retatrutide Phase 3 readout</a>.</p>

<p><strong>Glycaemic control.</strong> HbA1c reductions of 1.5–2.0 percentage points in type 2 diabetes populations.</p>

<p><strong>Cardiovascular outcomes.</strong> SELECT demonstrated MACE reduction with semaglutide in non-diabetic patients with obesity and established cardiovascular disease. This is the finding that moved these drugs from metabolic management into cardiovascular medicine.</p>

<p><strong>Emerging indications.</strong> Signals across sleep apnoea, MASH and chronic kidney disease programmes.</p>

<h2>Metabolic flexibility, defined</h2>

<p>Metabolic flexibility is the capacity to switch cleanly between fuel sources — oxidising fat when fasted or at low intensity, oxidising glucose when fed or working hard.</p>

<p>Flexible looks like: low fasting insulin, efficient post-meal glucose clearance, rising fat oxidation with fasting duration, preserved mitochondrial capacity.</p>

<p>Inflexible looks like the inverse: persistently elevated insulin, incomplete glucose clearance, blunted fat oxidation, reduced mitochondrial function. This is the pathology upstream of type 2 diabetes, fatty liver disease and most of the metabolic syndrome cluster.</p>

<p>The question is where GLP-1 therapy acts on that picture.</p>

<h2>The honest read</h2>

<p><strong>Insulin sensitivity improves substantially on therapy</strong> — but most of the improvement tracks with weight loss and reduced hepatic and pancreatic fat, not with a direct pharmacological effect on tissue insulin signalling.</p>

<p><strong>Fasting insulin and HOMA-IR fall,</strong> sometimes dramatically.</p>

<p><strong>Post-meal fat oxidation patterns in metabolic chamber studies don't show clean improvement</strong> in fuel-switching independent of body composition change.</p>

<p><strong>Mitochondrial function isn't consistently improved</strong> beyond what the weight loss and any accompanying activity would produce anyway.</p>

<p>The mechanistic frame that fits: these drugs reduce the metabolic load, allowing an underlying system to compensate more easily. Whether that system becomes more resilient is largely determined by what happens alongside the pharmacology — resistance training, aerobic base, sleep, diet quality.</p>

<p>The drug opens a window. What goes into the window determines whether it matters after it closes.</p>

<h2>The discontinuation problem</h2>

<p>STEP-4 and multiple follow-up studies show that stopping therapy after significant loss typically results in regaining most of it — often two-thirds or more — within twelve months.</p>

<p>The mechanism is unmysterious. Appetite suppression ends. The physiological drive to defend the previous fat mass returns. Behavioural inputs that weren't rebuilt during pharmacotherapy remain exactly as they were.</p>

<p>This isn't a criticism of the drugs. It's a description of what obesity pharmacology is. These are chronic disease management, closer analogically to antihypertensives than to a course of antibiotics. Nobody expects blood pressure to stay down after stopping a beta blocker.</p>

<p>Framing GLP-1 therapy as a time-limited intervention is a setup for the regain that follows, and a great deal of consumer marketing frames it exactly that way.</p>

<h2>The muscle loss question</h2>

<p>Rapid weight loss under any mechanism carries disproportionate lean mass loss. Trial data suggests 25–40% of weight lost on semaglutide or tirzepatide is lean tissue — roughly comparable to fast diet-based loss, but a real concern for long-term functional capacity, particularly in older adults.</p>

<p>This isn't a side effect in the pharmacological sense. It's weight loss physics. Losing mass quickly costs muscle unless something actively defends it.</p>

<p>The levers with reasonable evidence:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Protein intake</strong> at 1.6–2.2 g per kg of actual body weight, not target weight — <a href="https://biohackhealth.uk/blog/collagen-peptides-human-trials">collagen peptides</a> can supplement this but should not displace complete protein</li>
<li><strong>Resistance training</strong> at least twice weekly throughout the loss phase</li>
<li><strong>Slower titration</strong> where clinically appropriate</li>
</ul>

<p>None of these eliminate lean mass loss. They reduce the fraction.</p>

<h2>Editorial perspective</h2>

<p>These are among the most consequential pharmacological developments of the past decade for cardiometabolic disease. They also sit at the intersection of enormous demand, aggressive marketing and a clinical picture more nuanced than either supporters or critics tend to allow.</p>

<p><strong>Treating this as a shortcut gets the framing wrong.</strong> Regain after discontinuation isn't drug failure. It's how the physiology works, and it was predictable from the mechanism.</p>

<p><strong>The metabolic improvement is real but mostly load-mediated.</strong> What determines whether it outlasts the pharmacology is what happens alongside — training to preserve muscle, aerobic work to preserve mitochondrial capacity, sleep and diet quality to preserve insulin signalling.</p>

<p><strong>The compounded GLP-1 boom was an access failure, not a clinical strategy.</strong> With shortages resolved and compounded copies wound down — the timeline is covered in our <a href="https://biohackhealth.uk/blog/peptide-regulation-2026">peptide regulation piece</a> — the more important question is how these drugs get integrated into primary care at scale, not who can route around branded pricing.</p>

<p><strong>The triple agonists change the magnitude, not the logic.</strong> Retatrutide's Phase 3 numbers are outside anything previously reported — see the full readout. Every consideration above still applies, and the discontinuation question applies more forcefully, not less, because the loss is larger.</p>

<h2>Open questions</h2>

<ul>
<li>Long-term outcomes for triple agonists beyond the initial Phase 3 readouts</li>
<li>Standardised post-discontinuation transition protocols with behavioural scaffolding</li>
<li>Muscle preservation strategies during rapid pharmacological loss in mid-life and older adults</li>
<li>Interaction between GLP-1 pharmacology, sleep apnoea burden and cardiovascular outcomes</li>
<li>Effects on brain reward circuitry beyond food reward</li>
</ul>

<h2>Frequently asked</h2>

<div>
<h3>Do these drugs cure obesity?</h3>
<p>No. They manage it while the drug is on board. Discontinuation typically produces significant regain unless the underlying inputs have changed.</p>

<h3>Do they fix insulin resistance?</h3>
<p>They substantially improve the downstream metrics, largely via weight loss and reduced pancreatic and hepatic fat. Whether they improve intrinsic tissue insulin sensitivity independent of that is much less clear.</p>

<h3>Will I lose muscle?</h3>
<p>Some, yes. High protein intake and consistent resistance training reduce the fraction of loss that comes from lean tissue but don't eliminate it.</p>

<h3>How long can someone stay on them?</h3>
<p>The clinical framing is chronic management. Efficacy and tolerability data through three to four years is strong; longer-term data is still accumulating.</p>

<h3>Is retatrutide better than tirzepatide?</h3>
<p>It produces larger weight loss in cross-trial comparison — 28.3% at 80 weeks against approximately 21% at 72. No head-to-head Phase 3 exists, trial durations differ, and retatrutide isn't approved anywhere.</p>

<h3>Should GLP-1 therapy be combined with strength training?</h3>
<p>If muscle preservation matters — and it should — yes. This is the single highest-value adjunct behaviour during pharmacological weight loss.</p>

<h3>What happens if a dose is missed?</h3>
<p>For weekly agents, one missed dose is generally not clinically significant. Extended gaps function as a taper and may produce appetite return and regain.</p>
