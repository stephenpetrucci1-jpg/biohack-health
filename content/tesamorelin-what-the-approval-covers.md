Tesamorelin sits in an odd position. Almost every compound covered on this site
is preclinical, unapproved, or both. Tesamorelin is neither. It went through
two large placebo-controlled Phase 3 trials, it was approved by the FDA in
2010 under the name Egrifta, and it has been prescribed clinically for over a
decade.

That makes it the strongest evidence base of anything in the peptide
conversation. It also makes the gap between the evidence and the marketing
easier to see, because for once there is a real file to compare the claims
against.

## What the molecule is

Tesamorelin is a synthetic analogue of growth hormone releasing hormone. The
natural hormone is a 44 amino acid peptide made in the hypothalamus that
travels to the pituitary and triggers growth hormone release. Its problem, as
a drug, is that it is destroyed within minutes by the enzyme DPP-4.

Tesamorelin is that same sequence with a modification at one end that blocks
the enzyme. The result survives long enough to be injected once a day and
still reach the pituitary.

The distinction that matters is what it does not do. It is not growth hormone.
It does not put growth hormone into your body. It asks the pituitary to
release its own, which means output stays under the body's existing feedback
control. Levels rise, then the loop pulls them back. That is a meaningfully
different pharmacology from injecting growth hormone directly, and it is the
main argument for this class of compound.

## What the trials measured

Two 26-week placebo-controlled trials enrolled adults with HIV-associated
lipodystrophy, a condition in which antiretroviral therapy drives fat into the
abdominal cavity around the organs. That visceral fat is metabolically
active and associated with cardiovascular risk, which is why it was worth
treating.

The primary endpoint was visceral adipose tissue measured by CT scan.
Tesamorelin at 2 mg daily produced reductions in the region of 15 to 18
percent against placebo, sustained across the treatment period. IGF-1 rose
substantially, as the mechanism predicts. Later work in the same population
also showed reductions in liver fat.

Read the endpoint carefully. The trials measured a specific fat compartment in
a specific disease. They did not measure lean mass gain, strength, recovery
or training adaptation, and they were not run in healthy people.

## What happens when you stop

An extension phase answered a question most coverage skips. Participants who
came off tesamorelin regained the visceral fat. The compound suppresses the
compartment while it is being taken, and the suppression is not durable
afterwards.

This is the same pattern seen with the
[GLP-1 agonists](/blog/glp1-metabolic-flexibility/), and it points at the same
underlying truth. A drug that reduces a load does not necessarily change the
process generating the load. Stop the drug and the process resumes.

## The glucose problem

Growth hormone opposes insulin. That is basic endocrinology, not a surprising
finding, and it showed up in the trials as measurable worsening of glycaemic
parameters in some participants.

The effect was modest and it was reversible, but it points in the opposite
direction from the reason most people are interested in the compound. Someone
using tesamorelin to reduce abdominal fat for metabolic reasons is accepting a
drug that makes one metabolic marker worse in order to improve another. In the
approved population, where visceral fat carries a specific cardiovascular
risk, that trade was judged worthwhile. Outside it, nobody has run the study
that would tell you.

## Where the evidence stands

| Domain | Evidence | Confidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Visceral fat reduction in HIV lipodystrophy | Two Phase 3 RCTs, approved indication | High |
| IGF-1 elevation | Consistent and dose-related | High |
| Liver fat reduction in the same population | Controlled data | Moderate to high |
| Effect reverses on discontinuation | Extension data | High |
| Glucose tolerance worsens modestly | Trial data | Moderate to high |
| Lean mass gain in healthy trained adults | No controlled trial | None |
| Recovery, sleep or joint benefit | No controlled trial | None |
| Long-term safety beyond trial duration | Not established | Low |

The pattern is worth sitting with. The rows with high confidence are all
things the trials were designed to measure in the population they enrolled.
The rows with no evidence are the ones the bodybuilding coverage leads on.

## Where the dosing conventions come from

The 2 mg daily figure that circulates is not a bodybuilding protocol that
someone worked out. It is the approved clinical dose, lifted from the trials
and applied to a different population for a different purpose.

That does not make it wrong. It does make it an extrapolation rather than a
tested regimen, and the cycling schedules that get attached to it, the eight
to twelve week blocks and the post-cycle timings, have no trial behind them at
all. They are convention.

## How it compares within the class

Tesamorelin, [ipamorelin and CJC-1295](/blog/ipamorelin-tesamorelin-cjc-1295/)
get grouped together as growth hormone peptides, which obscures the thing that
actually separates them. Tesamorelin and CJC-1295 act on the GHRH receptor.
Ipamorelin acts on a different receptor entirely, the ghrelin receptor, and
mimics a different signal.

More to the point here: only one of the three has ever completed Phase 3 and
been approved. If evidence quality is what you care about, that is the whole
comparison.

## The legal position

Tesamorelin is approved, but the approval is for a named indication in a named
population, supplied on prescription. Material sold as research grade sits in
the separate channel covered in our
[peptide regulation piece](/blog/peptide-regulation-2026/): it is a laboratory
reagent, not a medicine, and it is not approved for human use in any
jurisdiction regardless of the fact that a prescription version of the same
molecule exists.

The existence of an approved product does not transfer its approval to
material bought through a different channel.

## Editorial perspective

Tesamorelin deserves more respect than most of this category and less
enthusiasm than it gets.

More respect, because the file is real. Two placebo-controlled Phase 3 trials
and a regulatory approval put it in a different class from compounds resting
on rodent studies and forum consensus. When it reduces visceral fat, that is a
measured finding, not an inference.

Less enthusiasm, because everything interesting about it to a general audience
sits outside what was studied. The trials enrolled people with a specific
drug-induced pathology. Whether a healthy trained adult with ordinary
abdominal fat responds the same way is not known, because the study has not
been run. The IGF-1 rise is real and gets used as a proxy for muscle benefit,
but a biomarker moving is not the same as an outcome improving, and the trials
that measured body composition were not measuring the thing lifters care
about.

The honest summary is that tesamorelin has strong evidence for one thing in
one population, weak evidence by extrapolation for anything else, and a
metabolic trade-off that gets left out of most of the coverage.

## Frequently asked

**Is tesamorelin approved?** Yes, for reduction of excess abdominal fat in
adults with HIV-associated lipodystrophy. Not for body composition,
performance or recovery in healthy adults.

**Does it build muscle?** No controlled trial in healthy trained adults has
tested this. IGF-1 rises, which is the mechanism people reason from, but
reasoning from a biomarker is not the same as evidence of an outcome.

**Is it safer than growth hormone?** The mechanism is arguably more
physiological, since output stays under pituitary feedback control rather than
being supplied from outside. That is an argument about mechanism, not a
demonstrated safety comparison, and no head-to-head trial exists.

**Does the fat come back?** In the extension data, yes. The reduction was not
maintained after discontinuation.

**What about the glucose effect?** Modest worsening of glycaemic measures was
seen in trials and is consistent with growth hormone's known action against
insulin. It reversed on stopping.
